The Tower is a videogame demo developed independently as a final project in university, with conception, planning, and development taking around eight months in total. The demo is approximately 2 hours in playtime, with rudimentary assets, mechanics, and content, focussing on realising the core idea behind the ambitious project within the short timeframe. My project (design & implementation) was awarded a grade of 85: an outstandingly well-designed ... fully-playable game that realises [my] conceptual goals while exhibiting technical ambition and strong production quality.

The demo is a stringing-together of excerpts from the first-act of what would be a longer game, placed organically within a currently sparse & ad hoc open-world.

The design, and all elements, were tested and revised sensitive to independent and user testing.

Design

The aim of the project was to realise and sustain a sensation of sublime in a 3d action roleplaying game. That is to say, the sense of awe and wonder felt by players of excellent roleplaying games. The efficacy of both The Elder Scrolls iv Oblivion (Bethesda Game Studios, 2006) and Dark Souls (FromSoftware, 2011) in this realm have been analysed by Martin (2011) and Vella (2015) respectively.

The approach taken begins with Vella's observation of obfuscation of a game's precise working supporting sublime's longevity; while I bear in mind throughout that if such is done too liberally, a game can be too obscure, having the opposite effect.

I intended all game aspects to fulfill this ambition, and for this purpose outlined three cornerstones I would adhere to for all features:

  • All features should expand and enrich possibility for interaction with the world, rather than limiting it.
  • The player should be able, through their own effort, to obtain the means to forge their own path through the game.
  • The exact behaviour of mechanics should remain mysterious.

There were many unique and interesting mechanics I implemented as a means of achieving my aim, not all of which I will discuss in order to protect my ideas. However there are minor yet very effective features that I will explain.

Lack of "class"/"build" system

An early decision, related to my intention that features should expand — not limit — interaction possibilities. Possiblities are not limited by choice of class.

Emphasis on items

Without arbitrary build restriction, all items can be used by all players, encouraging me to expand possibilities through items rather than levelling-up. Enemies dropping useful items can then be used as an insentive for combat given the removal of the insentive to level-up.

Save system & fast-travel

Given the emphasis on items, I did not want a punitive save system that discourages their use: e.g. failure in Dark Souls will restore a boss to full-health but not restore items wasted in the battle attempt.

Meanwhile, manual saving makes moment-to-moment gameplay less tense as there is no risk of loss of progress.

My solution is using Fountains as the only points at which progress is saved: death results in the entire game state returning to the point at when the last Fountain was interacted with.

Fountains are also the only points of fast-travel, and their functionality is explained in the lore.

Mutable world state

The world itself changes according to player actions — sometimes without clear causal link — influenced by World Tendency from Demon's Souls (FromSoftware, 2009) and Insight / Blood Moon from Bloodborne (FromSoftware, 2015).

Destructible environment pieces

A decision conducive to my design cornerstones was that the results of systems should be shown, not explained.

Destructible environment pieces provide new paths to observant players, while still being intuitable from the environment alone — avoiding the extreme obscurity of Dark Souls' illusory walls. They are also taught by the environment: the player is required to utilise the mechanic to escape their initial cell in the Prison. Thereon, the player associates such crumbling environments to destructability.

Moreover, destructible columns bridge two visible areas, so informing the player that visible yet seemingly inaccessible areas can be accessed by creative means, so broadening their conception of the game.

Sprinting & stamina

I deliberated over how sprinting should affect stamina. If sprinting steadily drained the stamina bar, it would be unsuitable for its purpose: crossing vast areas at speed; but an increase in speed should not be free.

I like the systems of The Elder Scrolls iii: Morrowind (Bethesda Game Studios, 2002) and Elden Ring (FromSoftware, 2022) wherein sprinting is free in the environment alone but has a penalty in combat. These systems however are unimmersive: it is not intuitive for sprinting to drain stamina yet continue to function without it in The Elder Scrolls iii: Morrowind, and Elden Ring's system of only draining stamina in combat feels ad hoc.

The Tower's system is better: there is a fixed stamina cost in the initial dash, and sprinting after that does not drain stamina but nor does it regenerate while the player continues to run. This makes sprinting costless while exploring but costly in combat — and without inbuilt inconsistency in the mechanic.

Invisible choices

Another invaluable element to sublime quest design is invisible choices, those being consequential actions that are not explicitly presented to the player as such, which are explained by Game Maker's Toolkit (2022). The clearest invisible choice is freeing the Dragon. This is not an option explicitly presented to the player, but one that requires a particular activity and prior preparation to be completed, with many individual "invisible" factors. But beyond this, there are even more invisible choices in the game, which have consequences far more difficult to place.

Useful inconveniences

Moreover, situations that inconvenience the player are deliberately formed, as they invoke the player to consider there being a more efficient solution. For example, the Moonstone Scythe is required to break the Dragon's chains —this weapon requires two hands to wield and so the player must forgo their torch in this area of pitch-darkness; also within the Prison however, a hood granting illuminated vision can be acquired from a mage.

This mindset of their being more advantageous strategies will then persist outside of these inconveniences to all gameplay moments: so the player becomes sublimely aware that their current experience is not summative of the game's possibilities.

Planning

All features were carefully considered with the purpose of achieving the sublime. These were implemented in rudimentary form, tested independently, then tested again in carefully designed user tests, to evaluate efficacy and inform the next development sprint within an agile methodology.

The initial planning approach was the early definition of a Minimum Viable Product (mvp) to establish the ideas in a small project, with a Gantt chart used to project and maintain progress.

However I found it useful to adopt a more agile and iterative approach, whereby I defined successive intermediate versions spaced one or two weeks apart, each having its own design goal, implementing features in a basic manner, being evaluated upon, sometimes with the assitance of other users, then informing the plan for the next intermediate version. The aim was to ensure both consistent progress, and that all progress was made with the final objective in mind.

Each intermediate version as far as the mvp was outlined first before starting development, with flexibility permitted. The Gantt chart continued to be used as an overarching monitor of progress.

In conjunction with development, a Task Tracker was used to formalise individual tasks, permitting also notes on bugs and opportunities for improvement and extension.

Development

The game was developed in the Godot Engine using the c# programming language.

To ensure congruency of elements, to advance flexibility and iteration, and with the final in-game quality in mind, I intended that as far as possible, development should occur in-engine. For example, atomic level components are created in Blender, then the levels put together in the engine.

I used Blender to model, texture, and animate characters, level-pieces, and items for the game; although I often created materials within Godot and assigned them to level pieces in-engine to ensure control, consistency across objects, and direct feedback of how the visuals look in-game.

While Godot encourages the use of its bespoke gdScript, I chose c# as my scripting language as it is strongly typed, faster, and more universal; it also, through its Node system, stimulates compositional practices that I have leveraged through my scripting, contriving a flexibility fit for a sublime game that resists formulae or simple, intuitable structures.

Specifically, an entity within the game can comprise as many of the implemented mechanics as I wish, rather than those mechanics being restricted by its class. This reflects that same quality of the game's lack of a build system.

Dialogue took special and careful implementation, enabling its interaction with many other game systems: dialogue can be affected by, and affect, any game attributes, while also being responsive to player input.

Level Design

The forefront consideration of world and level design was Vella's concept of "indistinct boundaries" extracted from Dark Souls' own world design, contrasted against the transparent openness of The Elder Scrolls iv: Oblivion's open world.

For the overall world structure, I decided that a truncated linear main path embellished by branching content befits a highly replayable game that allows the player to discover more content each playthrough. Meanwhile, individual locations are open.

To ensure the designing of areas consistent with the overarching aim, prior to sketching level layouts I listed level aims for the particular level in its own text document. This would also outline how the level fits into the rest of the game, and how it should impact the player emotionally. Therefore all areas contribute in their way to the gestalt sublime experience.

Moreover, moodboards were used for each area in order to capture a particular aesthetic.

Levels were then sketched, then roughly blocked-out using primitive 3d shapes for a sense of scale, space, and design, while being easy to reconfigure to achieve the perfect layout. When content with the layout following thorough testing, I would gradually increase the fidelity with more textures and 3d models. This fast, in-engine approach was useful for scaling objects, ensuring views were aesthetic and environments traversable, and quickly testing various ideas.

The Prison was the focus of the level design for the demo. It was inspired by the Prison of Hope in Demon's Souls (FromSoftware, 2009), with a similar atmosphere and tension, but with the addition of offering the player multiple routes according to their strengths. It also contains side-areas embellishing the world and narrative. The area is designed to simultaneously fit into and enhance the rest of the game.

User Tests

User tests were carried out throughout development to determine the success of features as parts of the entire system, and inform progression.

  • The best way to test whether the game achieves the sublime was to observe how the player interacts without clear direction.
  • Tests were conducted in-person so I could examine actions and responses.
  • Users filled out questionnaires afterwards so I could record their unobservable impressions.
  • From observation notes and questionnaire results, a formal assessment would be written for that stage of testing.
  • The new iteration would be informed by the results.

There were three formulations of the test, designed for evaluating different aspects of the project.

  1. Test for when the game is too obscure: how difficult are objecties to achieve even when the player is aware of their existence? For this test, prior to playing, I give the player a list of objectives.
  2. Test what entices the player, and how their experience is impacted by awareness of their limits of knowledge: how do players act without any direction? For this test, I first gave the players no guidance, then on a second occasion giving them a list of objectives.
  3. Authentic test: how does the player act, and what can they achieve, with only the vaguest direction? For this test, the only guidance was the suggestion to complete two quests.

I noted that in test Versions 1 & 2, where objectives were given, the user became objective-focussed and so missed content, assuming that the objectives given were indicative of a linear progression and so thinking they needed to be tackled in that order, and after missing some assuming that they had done something wrong; while in actuality there was a lot of flexibility in achieving the objectives with only some contingent on others, and many required thorough exploration of areas, which the objective-focussed mindset discouraged.

Challenges I Overcame

The main challenge within the game's concept was how to create a game that not only evokes a sense of sublime but has the capacity to sustain it, bearing in mind that game aspects should not be too obscure. I also had to work around the inherent insuitability of the subject towards user testing.

Many additions, revisions, and reductions were made to the game on the basis of the user tests, withal not permitting user feedback to muddy the existing vision, lest appeal to a wider audience forfeit appeal to the target audience. For example:

  • In early tests, I noticed that players were unlikely to pick up items from fallen enemies; I hypothesised that this was due to an absence of information presented about items within the inventory, which seemed to have the additional effect of making it more difficult for players to discern new items from those already acquired. I decided to reveal more useful information about items in the inventory ui, and found that this did encourage players to more often acquire these items. While I could test the effects of revealing more detailed information about items, I want to encourage acquiring items because of inherent curiosity rather than a statistical judgement about which is best — with is partly why I did not enforce a limit on how much the player is able to pick up. So I continued to reveal variable amounts of attribute information in tests.
  • Some players did not experiment with different kinds of weapons. I effectively remedied this without the revealing of weapon information by diversifying combat situations and so more strongly, but still implicitly, encouraging the player to consider using a weapon appropriate for the situation. I also found that many players, by contrast, were excited by finding new weapons that by design looked more powerful: that players can infer strength without seeing the attributes allows me to avoid the game becoming a formulaic game of numbers and instead encouraging attention to the details of the presented world.
  • It was evident that the Prison encouraged exploration moreso than Gatetown, because of its non-linear layout with an uncertain objective location, and having the presence of interesting encounters early on to pique enduring explorative interest. While such features are ultimately expected for Gatetown too, I decided to gradually implement them from this point to test their efficacy towards sublimity. Nevertheless, it is important that the player is not prompted to engage with all the content, so it was a good outcome when players explored but did not find everything possible. I did consider that some players were misjudging the game as a straightforward action game and so only focussing on a singular main objective. I therefore frustrated the player's journey through Gatetown: since doing this, players explore more, and become wisened to the overall complexity of mechanics and interactions within the game.
  • I found that more hints and context were necessary in the "Amelia" and "Dragon" quests. This was done in the form of more characters and hidden areas, which further enlarge the world and reward exploration. Since implementing these, players found the flow of these quests more natural and were able to construct paths through them. This solved the issue without compromising the game's overall sublimity.

Some changes were deliberately not made, in order to preserve the game's identity. For example, a player indicated desire for an in-game map of the Prison, while also noting the feeling of being lost. The fact is, the feeling of being lost is an objective of the Prison's design, which would not be so strongly felt with a map; so small inconveniences to the player are sometimes necessary in order to achieve a greater total effect. An in-game map is a marker of the "completion" of exploration within an area, which is absolutely antithetical to sublime design. In regards to a suitable balance, I should focus on having areas that are not so confusing that the absence of a map causes more harm than good, and this is a feature that must be balanced with the indistinct boundaries.

Pitch

In the ideation phase, I developed an abstract summary of the concept for review from and discussion with a supervisor. This consisted of my basic idea drawn from my research and experience, including a brief literature review, with suggestions of how to develop it.

Towards the end of the assigned period I pitched my demo in its current state. This involved a presentation, wherein I:

  1. Introduce the concept, i.e. the problem being solved.
  2. Outline my approach to solving the problem, through particular features and development practices.
  3. Demonstrate features through a small video, over which I comment.
  4. Evaluate on the successes and challenges so far, scalability, and path going forwards.

Reflection

The individual implemented design featurs succeeded towards the project's aim, as revealed in the user tests; though whether the project as a whole will achieve the aim remains to be tested, requiring expansion of the project to test features' scalability.

The emotional effect of the Prison, in its atmosphere and level design, was particularly successful throughout all tests. Players enjoyed its size, which is first hidden from the player, until further exploration; players also enjoyed the Prison's secrets: it is clear the Prison's design is succesfully evocative of the sublime.

Players were consistently most excited when discovering hidden elements and making progress in quests. This always paired with a sense of surprise, which exists only because of the inexplicit nature of all these things. I do not believe that the feeling of excitement would be as great if the path to these things was straighforward.

I found it difficult to test how well the longevity of the sublime is supported owing to the short test sessions. The best I could do in this regard was to have some users test multiple times. There were then conclusions that could be exclusively drawn from these tests. I did find that when players returned to the game a second time, they always discovered things that they had not previously, and were excited by them.

Nevertheless, the issue sustained pertaining to the limited scale and content of the game, that is not on the surface indicative of a sublime experience, so affecting the player's mode of interaction. Results of the questionnaire exposed that players often viewed the world as a singular and immutable crafted unit rather than an unfathomable and dynamic sublime whole. This was combatted by adding more content at the start of the game that the player is likely to encounter, with these all intending to open up the mechanical scope of the game. When the same player played multiple different times, they were excited by the emergence of new events, which they had not discovered before.

Players had different natural inclinations. The questionnaire reveals that this might be due to their exposure to different kinds of games, which value different styles of play. Given the same build and same objective, some players would focus entirely on entering the Tower, and others would carefully explore Gatetown. This is a good outcome as it allows observant players to be specially rewarded. These dispositions informed by different game experiences could help or hinder their interaction with the game; I hypothesise that many players tried to apply their existing mental models from other games to this one, which could make exploration more or less effective. But by interrupting this model with the addition of new events in the earliest sections of the game I found that players' approaches shifted to be more explorative and consider the world as less linear.

Going forwards, I am interested in verifying that the sublime can be further elevated by adding more content and extending the existing mechanics. Indeed the features utilised by the game seem to progress towards that end, and from the tests it is strongly suggested they would suffice for a sublime game given enough time and resources.
