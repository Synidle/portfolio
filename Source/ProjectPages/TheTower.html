The Tower is a videogame demo developed independently as a final project in university, with conception, planning, and development taking around eight months in total. The demo is approximately two hours in playtime, with rudimentary assets, mechanics, and content, with the focus of realising the core idea behind the ambitious project within the short timeframe.

Design

The aim of the project was to realise and sustain a sensation of sublime in a 3D action roleplaying game. That is to say, the sense of awe and wonder felt by players of excellent roleplaying games. The efficacy of both The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion (Bethesda Game Studios, 2006) and Dark Souls (FromSoftware, 2011) in this realm has been analysed by Martin (2011) and Vella (2015) respectively; and the foundation for this project draws upon that literature whilst critiquing and developing upon it. To achieve this aim, I was prepared to question and forego design conventions as was sensible according to hypotheses, and then rigorously test the effect of design decisions with real users.

The approach taken adopts Vella's observation of obfuscation of a game's precise working supporting sublime's longevity, while recognising that if such is done too liberally it has the opposite effect.

There were many unique and interesting mechanics I implemented as a means of achieving my aim, not all of which I will discuss in order to protect my ideas. However there are minor yet very effective features that I will explain.

One way in which The Tower achieves sublimity while avoiding extreme obscurity is in its destructible environment pieces. Dark Souls and Elden Ring (FromSoftware, 2022) hide areas, so expanding their worlds beyond that visible, by using illusory walls: walls appearing normal, but on interaction disappearing. In offline mode, there is no indication a wall is illusory, nor even that they exist; yet the player messages left in online mode make them too transparent and so effectively redundant, while also offering other player advice that can diminish the sense of discovery. The player essentially has to pick the lesser of these two "evils" — both of which hurt the ability to effectuate sublimity. In The Tower, destructible environment pieces are similarly used to hide areas or connexion points, but in a manner not too transparent nor to obscure. The player is most likely sent to the Prison early in the game, wherein they are required to destroy a wall to progress, with there being implicit indicators that the wall can be destroyed. This awakens the player to the mechanic, which they associate with the crumbling environment they begin the Prison section in —and so in other such environments of the Prison, there are other destructible elements. Others are less explicit, but in no case could the player never have possibly suspected that the element could be destroyed. Destructible columns also offer a similar yet different experience, by bridging two visible areas, and so informing the player that visible yet seemingly inaccessible areas can be accessed by creative means, so broadening their conception of the game in much the same way.

Another consideration was how to save. The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion etc. allow the player to manually save at any moment, which trivialises many gameplay aspects, and makes moment-to-moment gameplay less tense, with less emotional investment. Meanwhile, Dark Souls etc. use particular checkpoints. The problem with Dark Souls' system is that the game state is saved continuously, with only the player's position returning to the checkpoint, and minor enemies returning. So items consumed before dying remain consumed after respawning. I find that this discourages the use of items, and by extension experimentation. My solution was using Fountains as the only points at which progress is saved, also using them as points of fast-travel to other Fontains to facilitate back-and-forth in the open-world. Refer back to my commitment that all mechanics should expand rather than restrict the player's conception of possibility within the game: allowance of experimentation comes under this.

Unplanned mechanics were introduced as testing revealed them necessary. For example, I initiaully did not want to have a "running" movement option because I find different movement speeds are often redundant features, and I do not want the player to rush through the game; however in order to have such large environments I would need to grant the plaeyr faster movement. I did not want to just increase the base movement as this would make the player too fast in tight areas; I also did not want to introduce additional input complexity with a new feature so I linked the run with the existing dash mechanic. There was then the issue of how running should affect stamina. Given running existed to allow the player to efficiently cross large environments, a draining stamina gauge should not inhibit this, but also an increase of speed should not be free. I like the systems of The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind (Bethesda Game Studios, 2002) and Elden Ring where running is free in the environment alone but has a penalty in combat. These systems however are unimmersive: it is not intuitive for running to drain stamina yet continue to function without stamina in The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind, and Elden Ring's system of only draining stamina in combat feels ad hoc. My system is better: there is a fixed stamina cost in the initial dash, and running after that does not drain stamina but nor does it regenerate while the player continues to run. This makes running costless while exploring but costly in combat — and without inbuilt inconsistency in the mechanic.

These were implemented in rudimentary form, tested independently, then tested again in carefully designed user tests, to evaluate efficacy and inform the next development sprint within an agile methodology.

Level Design

The forefront consideration of world and level design was Vella's concept of "indistinct boundaries" extracted from Dark Souls' own world design, contrasted against the transparent and liberal openness of The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion's open world.

I intuited from the literature and my own experiences of games that a world seemingly linear that branches out, with difficulty, and only at close inspection, is more sublime, and more able to surprise and inspire wonder, than a world that is transparently open, so laying all its cards down at once. Furthermore, a truncated linear main path embellished by branching content befits a highly replayable game that allows the player to discover more content each playthrough and tread paths that they did not on their first playthrough, perhaps because they were "evil" — i.e. discouraged in the narrative —but which nevertheless seemed interesting. Replayability is necessary for a sublime game: a game is not leveraging sublimity if the player, having finished the game once, is aware that they missed content, but is not inclined to play the game again to encounter new content; a game is most sublime if a player can continue returning, discovering new content, and so making the concept of the game in their mind seemingly infinite.

It would seem obvious for a sublime game to have a huge world, and a lengthy, epic story; but long main-questlines usually have only one route through, or very minor variations thereupon. The Tower aims to have completely different paths through the main story depending upon the factions joined, which is possible with a shorter story. This makes a single playthrough far less representative of the game's scope of possibility. This feeds into the game's consistent encouragement of replay, allowing new events to emerge and so expanding the sublime horizon observed by the player.

To ensure the designing of areas consistent with the overarching aim, prior to sketching level layouts I listed level aims for the particular level in its own text document. This would also outline how the level fits into the rest of the game, and how it should impact the player emotionally. Therefore all areas contribute in their way to the gestalt sublime experience. Moreover, moodboards were uesd for each area in order to capture a particular aesthetic.
