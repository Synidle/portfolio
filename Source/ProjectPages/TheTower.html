The Tower is a videogame demo developed independently as a final project in university, with conception, planning, and development taking around eight months in total. The demo is approximately two hours in playtime, with rudimentary assets, mechanics, and content, with the focus of realising the core idea behind the ambitious project within the short timeframe. The project part of the assessment (design & implementation) was awarded a grade of 85 (an "outstandingly well-designed ... fully-playable game" that "realises [my] conceptual goals while exhibiting technical ambition and strong production quality").

Design

The aim of the project was to realise and sustain a sensation of sublime in a 3D action roleplaying game. That is to say, the sense of awe and wonder felt by players of excellent roleplaying games. The efficacy of both The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion (Bethesda Game Studios, 2006) and Dark Souls (FromSoftware, 2011) in this realm has been analysed by Martin (2011) and Vella (2015) respectively; and the foundation for this project draws upon that literature whilst critiquing and developing upon it. To achieve this aim, I was prepared to question and forego design conventions as was sensible according to hypotheses, and then rigorously test the effect of design decisions with real users.

The approach taken adopts Vella's observation of obfuscation of a game's precise working supporting sublime's longevity, while recognising that if such is done too liberally it has the opposite effect.

The design was commended for its strong contextualisation and theoretical background, and its well-reasoned justification for all design decisions; moreover for the design's proof of iterative improvement through cycles of testing.

There were many unique and interesting mechanics I implemented as a means of achieving my aim, not all of which I will discuss in order to protect my ideas. However there are minor yet very effective features that I will explain.

One way in which The Tower achieves sublimity while avoiding extreme obscurity is in its destructible environment pieces. Dark Souls and Elden Ring (FromSoftware, 2022) hide areas, so expanding their worlds beyond that visible, by using illusory walls: walls appearing normal, but on interaction disappearing. In offline mode, there is no indication a wall is illusory, nor even that they exist; yet the player messages left in online mode make them too transparent and so effectively redundant, while also offering other player advice that can diminish the sense of discovery. The player essentially has to pick the lesser of these two "evils" � both of which hurt the ability to effectuate sublimity. In The Tower, destructible environment pieces are similarly used to hide areas or connexion points, but in a manner not too transparent nor to obscure. The player is most likely sent to the Prison early in the game, wherein they are required to destroy a wall to progress, with there being implicit indicators that the wall can be destroyed. This awakens the player to the mechanic, which they associate with the crumbling environment they begin the Prison section in �and so in other such environments of the Prison, there are other destructible elements. Others are less explicit, but in no case could the player never have possibly suspected that the element could be destroyed. Destructible columns also offer a similar yet different experience, by bridging two visible areas, and so informing the player that visible yet seemingly inaccessible areas can be accessed by creative means, so broadening their conception of the game in much the same way.

Another consideration was how to save. The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion etc. allow the player to manually save at any moment, which trivialises many gameplay aspects, and makes moment-to-moment gameplay less tense, with less emotional investment. Meanwhile, Dark Souls etc. use particular checkpoints. The problem with Dark Souls' system is that the game state is saved continuously, with only the player's position returning to the checkpoint, and minor enemies returning. So items consumed before dying remain consumed after respawning. I find that this discourages the use of items, and by extension experimentation. My solution was using Fountains as the only points at which progress is saved, also using them as points of fast-travel to other Fontains to facilitate back-and-forth in the open-world. Refer back to my commitment that all mechanics should expand rather than restrict the player's conception of possibility within the game: allowance of experimentation comes under this.

Unplanned mechanics were introduced as testing revealed them necessary. For example, I initiaully did not want to have a "running" movement option because I find different movement speeds are often redundant features, and I do not want the player to rush through the game; however in order to have such large environments I would need to grant the plaeyr faster movement. I did not want to just increase the base movement as this would make the player too fast in tight areas; I also did not want to introduce additional input complexity with a new feature so I linked the run with the existing dash mechanic. There was then the issue of how running should affect stamina. Given running existed to allow the player to efficiently cross large environments, a draining stamina gauge should not inhibit this, but also an increase of speed should not be free. I like the systems of The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind (Bethesda Game Studios, 2002) and Elden Ring where running is free in the environment alone but has a penalty in combat. These systems however are unimmersive: it is not intuitive for running to drain stamina yet continue to function without stamina in The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind, and Elden Ring's system of only draining stamina in combat feels ad hoc. My system is better: there is a fixed stamina cost in the initial dash, and running after that does not drain stamina but nor does it regenerate while the player continues to run. This makes running costless while exploring but costly in combat � and without inbuilt inconsistency in the mechanic.

An issue with the game succeeding The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind is the use of quest-markers and too explicit directions, which dissolves the uncertainty of ways in which to progress a quest, and also discouraging interaction with the world in favour of directly pursuing the quest-marker. Quests in The Tower are therefore made navigable using diegetic information and player intuition alone. But acknowledging that a degree of obscurity is necessary for a sublime game, some quests are made harder to navigate than those common in The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind. For instance, there are no explicit directions in the quest related to the Dragon, and no indication that the Dragon Covenant Claw is the key to unlock the Dragon Gaol except for the subtle environment hint of the markings on the pedestal also appearing on the claw. A necessary consequence is that the player might chase red herrings: by following their own intuition the player is likely to suspect the existence of quests or paths that do not exist. However the possibility of this is necessary in the sublime experience, even if an undesirable consequence.

Another invaluable element to sublime quest design is invisible choices, those being consequential actions that are not explicitly presented to the player as such, which are explained by Game Maker's Toolkit (2022). The clearest invisible choice is freeing the Dragon. This is not an option explicitly presented to the player, but one that requires a particular activity and prior preparation to be completed, with many individual "invisible" factors: first finding the Dragon, of whom little is mentioned in-game, and acquiring the key to activate the door; then finding a weapon capable of cutting the chain; and finally the outcome is determined by the player's dialogue with the Dragon and certain attribute values, and whether the player possesses the Dragon Covenant Claw that enables allyship with the Dragon. But beyond this, there are even more invisible choices in hte game, which have consequences far more difficult to place.

Moreover, situations that inconvenience the player are deliberately formed, as they invoke the player to consider there being a more efficient solution. For example, the Moonstone Scythe is required to break the Dragon's chains �this weapon requires two hands to wield and so the player must forgo their torch in this area of pitch-darkness; also within the Prison however, a hood granting illuminated vision can be acquired from a mage, and this can be known to the player as the mage's line of vision is clearly illuminated. This mindset of their being more advantageous strategies will then persist outside of these inconveniences to all gameplay moments: so the player becomes sublimely aware that their current experience is not summative of the game's possibilities.

Planning

The game's world, story, unique mechanics, and so forth, were all carefully considered with the purpose of achieving the sublime. These were implemented in rudimentary form, tested independently, then tested again in carefully designed user tests, to evaluate efficacy and inform the next development sprint within an agile methodology.

The initial planning approach was the early definition of a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) to establish the ideas in a small project, with a Gantt chart used to project and maintain progress. However I found it useful to adopt a more agile and iterative approach, whereby I defined successive intermediate versions spaced one or two weeks apart, each having its own design goal, implementing features in a basic manner, being evaluated upon, sometimes wiht the assitance of other users, then informing the plan for the next intermediate version. The aim was to ensure both consistent progress, and that all progress was made with the fianl goal in mind. Each intermediate version as far as the MVP was outlined first before starting development, with flexibility permitted. The Gantt chart continued to be used as an overarching monitor of progress.

In conjunction with development, a Task Tracker was used to formalise individual tasks, permitting also notes on bugs and opportunities for improvement and extension.

Development

The game was developed in the Godot Engine using the C# programming language. While Godot encourages the use of its bespoke GDScript, I chose C# as my scripting language as it is strongly typed, faster, and more universal.

A notable reason for choosing Godot is that its Node system stimulates compositional practices. Furthermore, my scripting approach favoured composition over inheritance, which affords a greater flexibility perfect for a sublime game that resists formulae or simple, intuitable structures. Specifically, an entity within the game can comprise as many of the implemented mechanics as I wish, rather than those mechanics being restricted by its class. This reflects that same quality of the game's lack of a build system.

For example, having Door and Lock as separate classes, both of which reference but do not inherit the Interactable class, enables more possibilities regarding what can be locked and how they can be nlocked. This inables the disconnect between the door leading to the Dragon Gaol and its activator that requires a key, with the activator subtly hinting the requirement of the Covenant Claw: the door itself gives no suggestion of requiring a special keya nd so would leave the player confused, especially considering how many keys are in the game. The fact that the clase is not a conventional key means it will not unlock a conventional door, so the astute player will think of this activator.

When facing the issue of how to allow the player to save their progress at the start of the Prison while not having that Fountain checkpoint offer transportation out of the Prison at this stage, but allowing it later for the benefit of returning for quests, I made use of this mechanic again in another context: having the transportation capabilities of the fontain "locked" until external activation.

Dialogue is facilitated by a sequence of DialogueLines adn DialogueChoices that is dynamically determined during play according to player input and attribute values. The principal variable of dialogue is DialogueInt, which determines where to try to find the next DialogueLine, returning the first line at that index with a satisfied condition. The progress of dialogue is managed by setting this value. DialogueLines then display according to values of the player's attributes, the non-player character's attributes, inventory state, global attributes, and global Booleans. Checking inventory state includes checking for the presence of an item, a particular quantity of an item, or even the item's own attributes. Where DialogueChoices exist, they can display or be hidden according to the same attributes, and according to those same attributes they assess success or failure and then change the DialogueInt accordingly. DialogueLines and DialogueChoices can also update all the mentioned attributes. The player's persuasion attribute can be checked and compared to the non-player character's temperament attribute, and an optional additional modifier, to determine success or failure in dialogue, enabling further complexity and so sublimity in the perceived uncertainty of dialogue. All these values related to dialogue can be set in Godot's inspector.

Scene-switching and saving were also challenges to imiplement. A SceneManager singleton dealt with scene-switching, requiring that each scene capable of being independently loaded have both a GameScene script and a SceneResource. The SceneResource holds volatile data written during play; both SceneResource and GameScene reference the file-path of the scene so that the scene can be processed without a catastrophic circular reference. The movement of characters between scenes is facilitated by adding them to dictionaries in SceneResources, recording which characters should be added to or removed frmo scenes (compared to the default) when the scene is loaded. Scenes are only actually loaded or unloaded if the player moves between scenes; otherwise there is merely a change in data. GameScenes also store dictionaries associating EntrancePoints with string IDs; Doors also reference these IDs, as well as scene paths: as such scene-transfer can occur between EntrancePoints.

Loading scenes through SceneManager is asynchronous, enabling the loading of transitionary scenes while the target scene loads � usually the loading-screen scene, but the game introduction scene and the game-over scene are also loaded in this way. Issues arise with sending the player to the scene they currently occupy, which commonly happens if the player dies or uses Fountains to fast-travel. As such, the FountainManager singleton can translate the player within the same scene where required, and when the player dies they are transported to an "afterlife" scene temporarily.

The question of how to implement a save system posed a challenge given the number of entities in the game and the number of entity components needing to save their own data. My solution allows data to be saved according to the relevant component, and associated with a character. Saving is facilitated by a SaveManager singleton. It was decided what data specifically needs to be saved, and this data relates to a number of classes: for example, Dialogue. For each of these classes, a new class was made to encapsulate that data that should be saved, in addition to a CharacterId; the main class would then instantiate the data class and write it when the object is loaded, according to default values or what can be loaded from the SaveManager. Before the object is unloaded, the data class is overwritten and sent to the SaveManager to be recalled when the object is lodaed again. Classes for which data can be saved all implement and ISaveable interface to ensure compatibility with SaveManager.

Another issue I needed to resolve was the efficient assignment of values to naturally-pairing data, as a means of streamlining development � crucial for a project of this scale. Withi code, this is simply facilitated by dictionaries, as they allow pairing and efficient access. However, Godot has issues with exporting dictionaries to the inspector; Godot's own dictionary structure can be exported, but I found it unreliable due to it not being properly serialisable, causing it to often lose references; moreover Godot's own dictionaries are slower than System dictionaries as they translate their data to variant types, making them essentially untyped. My solution was to write serialisable Resource classes for pairing data, and then export arrays of these to the inspector rather than dictionaries. This enabled me to fill complex data in the inspector without risk of references being lost. I then wrote a generic utility method capable of converting any Resource array into a typed System dictionary that would allow fast access at runtime, with the only caveat being the initial writing of this dictionary would introduce an upfront time cost. That dictionaries can be accessed in O(1) time made it worth the conversion as their frequent need to be accessed ultmiately makes them faster than O(n) access of an array for which the key types would not translate to indices, so requiring the array to be searched rather than directly accessed. Consequently, the introduction of further complex data relationships will not adversely affect the game's efficiency. Becaues it was most efficient for me to use enum values in many cases, I created another method, utilising the first, tailored specifically to associate enum data with another type.

Level Design

The forefront consideration of world and level design was Vella's concept of "indistinct boundaries" extracted from Dark Souls' own world design, contrasted against the transparent and liberal openness of The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion's open world.

I intuited from the literature and my own experiences of games that a world seemingly linear that branches out, with difficulty, and only at close inspection, is more sublime, and more able to surprise and inspire wonder, than a world that is transparently open, so laying all its cards down at once. Furthermore, a truncated linear main path embellished by branching content befits a highly replayable game that allows the player to discover more content each playthrough and tread paths that they did not on their first playthrough, perhaps because they were "evil" � i.e. discouraged in the narrative �but which nevertheless seemed interesting. Replayability is necessary for a sublime game: a game is not leveraging sublimity if the player, having finished the game once, is aware that they missed content, but is not inclined to play the game again to encounter new content; a game is most sublime if a player can continue returning, discovering new content, and so making the concept of the game in their mind seemingly infinite.

It would seem obvious for a sublime game to have a huge world, and a lengthy, epic story; but long main-questlines usually have only one route through, or very minor variations thereupon. The Tower aims to have completely different paths through the main story depending upon the factions joined, which is possible with a shorter story. This makes a single playthrough far less representative of the game's scope of possibility. This feeds into the game's consistent encouragement of replay, allowing new events to emerge and so expanding the sublime horizon observed by the player.

To ensure the designing of areas consistent with the overarching aim, prior to sketching level layouts I listed level aims for the particular level in its own text document. This would also outline how the level fits into the rest of the game, and how it should impact the player emotionally. Therefore all areas contribute in their way to the gestalt sublime experience. Moreover, moodboards were uesd for each area in order to capture a particular aesthetic.

Levels were then sketched, then roughly blocked-out using primitive 3D shapes for a sense of scale, space, and design, while being easy to reconfigure to achieve the perfect layout. When content with the layout following thorough testing, I would gradually increase the fidelity with more textures and 3D models. For example, I was able to pull out the bridge leading into the Tower, making the outline of the town more interesting. Such a change was easy given the town was in the early stages of blocking-out, but would have been difficult were the town already high-fidelity. Moreover, I was able to easily introduce new interconnexions of paths in the Prison.

Modelling

Blender 4.3 was used for modelling and animation, and Aseprite, Krita, and Paint.NET for textures.

The game employs a retro early-3D aesthetic for numerous reasons. The association with classic RPGs implicitly conjures expectations of less "hand-holding". Furthermore, given the game's scale, lower-fidelity assets both permits production of more assets within a shorter time and the presence of more assets for less computational power. Finally, lower-quality assets more disconncted from realistic representation greater ignite the player's imagination; for this reason a low resolution is also used for a "grainy" effect that disguises model and textural imperfections � this is used in conjunction with a subtle noise filter. It is great that the player can observe distant locations and be able to wonder what they are, rather than instantly being able to make out details.

The choice to use a retro 4:3 aspect ratio does more than just provide authenticity to the style: it should improve the gameplay experience. The visual display is the primary means of communication from the game world to the player, particularly regarding world and level design; hence the player's subjectivity of the game is informed by the organisation of assets within the immediate display. A widescreen display is appropriate for most open-world or action games � particularly those that are in third-person �as it shows those features around the player horizontally; but in the case of The Tower, there is a very central focus, that being a vertical tower: an aspect ratio narrower than that of our eyes forces more focus on what is above and below the player, implicitly supporting vertical level design. A widescreen aspect ratio would encourage a more traditional open world game akin to The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion rather than encouraging a focussed, vertical, indistinct level design akin to Dark Souls.

Furthermore the game targets 30 frames per second rather than the standard 60 or higher in order to reinforce an aversion to strict combat execution challenge. I want the game to be more conceptually and intellectually challenging, and high skill requirements are another cause for the player to seek external help through guides in order to find manageable routes to overcome the challenge, causing a dissolution of the sublime by making explicit game information.

User Tests

User test were carried out throughout development to determine the success of features and inform progression. It was assumed that the easiest way to test for the sublime was to observe how the player interacts with the environment without clear directions and without knowing the possibilities, and then recording their thoughts afterwards. I wanted to analyse the fecundity of all aspects in regards to sublimity: the world design, mechanics, story and aesthetics, et cetera.

These tests were conducted in-person, so that I could examine the player's actions and responses, and what they were able to achieve and how difficult this was; this was supplemented with questionnaires sent to players, to collect data not discernible from observation. Notes would be made during the observation, and for each phase of testing, the notes and questionnaire results would be used to construct a formal assessment of that game build, including a specific list of tasks and insights going forwards. After all tests prior to the current prototype had been completed, a final document was written drawing from all of these, as a final evaluation of the project informed by testing.

Because I want the sublime to be established regardless of experience with games, I diversified my test subjects. It was apparent that players with different backgrounds in videogames had different approaches to the demo; to factor this into evaluation, I asked players about their gaming experience in the questionnaire.

There were three formulations of the test, designed for evaluating different aspects of the project. Version 1 was designed to test how difficult objectives are to achieve even with the player being aware of their existence, so revealing where the game is too obscure; in this test, prior to engagement with the prototype, players are shown a list of objectives with a particular number of points indicating how significant the objective is. Version 2 aimed at testing both how the player would act without any direction, and then what effect an awareness of the limits of their knowledge would have; in this test, players were first asked simply to explore, afterwards being given a list of tasks and asked to complete one they did not in their first time playing. I was interested to see which possible events would entice the player. Version 3 tested how the user would play and what they would be able to achieve with just the vaguest amount of external direction; the only guidance given to the player was the suggestion to "complete two quests".

The notes include identified errors, actions and discoveries made by the player, the reactions of the player to things, and any interesting comments made by the player.

Evaluation was done of the testing method itself. I found that the observation was the most important aspect of the testing, with the questionnaire useful as a supplement offering personal insight such as criticism and expectations, and impressions after reflection, and as a chance to contextualise the test according to the player's other experiences. However most of the multiple-choice questions were indecisive or contradicted the observation, which I trust more.

I noted that in test Versions 1 & 2, where objectives were given, the user became objective-focussed and so missed content, assuming that the objectives given were indicative of a linear progression and so thinking they needed to be tackled in that order, and after missing some assuming that they had done something wrong; while in actuality there was a lot of flexibility in achieving the objectives with only some contingent on others, and many required thorough exploration of areas, which the objective-focussed mindset discouraged.

In early tests, I noticed that players were unlikely to pick up items from falled enemies; I hypothesised that this was due to an absence of information presented about items within the inventory, which seemed to have the additional effect of making it more difficult for players to discern new items from those already acquired. I decided to reveal more useful information about items in the inventory UI, and found that this did encourage players to more often acquire these items. While I could test the effects of revealing more detailed information about items, I had to recognise the possibility of enabling the player to reduce the game to comparisons of numbers, so eliminating sublimity. Some judgements about items can be mdae by experimenting with them, and I want to encourage acquiring items because of inherent curiosity rather than a statistical judgement about which is best � with is partly why I did not enforce a limit on how much the player is able to pick up. So I continued to reveal variable amounts of attribute information in tests. Currently the game presentes itself in such a way that the user is encouraged to interact with it on their terms and always wonder how their experience could differ if they make different decisions.

Some players did not experiment with different kinds of weapons. I effectively remedied this without the revealing of weapon informaiton by diversifying combat situations and so more strongly, but still implicitly, encouraging the player to consider using a weapon appropriate for the situation. I also found that many players, by contrast, were excited by finding new weapons that by design looked more powerful: that players can infer strength without seeing the attributes allows me to avoid the game becoming a formulaic game of numbers and instead encouraging attention to the details of the presented world.

CHALLENGES I OVERCAME

The main challenge within the game's concept was how to create a game that not only initiates a sense of sublime but has the capacity to sustain it, bearing in mind the point of game aspects not being too obscure.

There were also many weaknesses of the game that became apparent through user tests, that I needed to solve without compromising the game's vision.

The working of destructible objects, in early user tests, did not seem to have been made clear, so I iteratively improved the way they are presented to hte player early in the Prison level in order to implant an awareness of them that might be drawn upon later. The issue is that making the mechanic explicit would create the expectation that all mechanics are explicitly revealed, and so harm the sublime impact of the game. Ultimately all players noted destructible walls, and once destructible pillars were made more prevalent most players noted them too; players appreciated the subtle difference in design and expressed their preference for these over the illusory walls of Dark Souls.

It was evident that the Prison encouraged exploration moreso than Gatetown, because of its non-linear layout with an uncertain objective location, and having the presence of interesting encounters early on to pique enduring explorative interest. Whie such features are ultimately expected for Gatetown too, I decided to gradually implement them from this point to test their efficacy towards sublimity. Nevertheless, it is important that the playe is not prompted to engage with all the content, so it was a good outcome when players explored but did not find everything possible. I did consider that some players were misjudging the game as a straightforward action game and so only focussing on a singular main objective. I therefore frustrated the player's journey through Gatetown: since doing this, players explore more, and become wisened to the overall complexity of mechanics and interactions within the game.

While players acted according to their natural intrigue, with the desired effect of them discovering some interactions but not all of them, there are some interactions that I want all players to be aware of that some missed. Notably, the Fountain in Gatetown. I decided to introduce this mechanic far earlier, making a Fountain the first object of interest upon entering the open world, rather than the crucial first Fountain being surrounded by other objects of interest and so potentially missed. I found that after this, players no longer missed the Fountain in Gatetown, and also immediately intuited how it worked in relation to the Fountain discovered earlier. The Fountain at the start better operates as a point that can be teleported back to later, whereas players were confused why the Fountain in Gatetown did not yet offer anything when being the first Fountain activated as it had nowhere yet to link to, especially considering the relative visual importance of Gatetown's Fountain. It also suggests that there are things of interest near the first Fountain, so reason to travel back, without making this explicit. Players then went on to reliably interact with other Fountains in the game. The fact that this arrangement worked means that I do not have to consider making Fountains more explicit as interactable elements � e.g. literally highlighting them � which would break the immersion and sublimity by framing the game as a composition of specific game elements.

I found that more hints and context were necessary in the "Amelia" and "Dragon" quests. This was done in the form of more characters and hidden areas, which further enlarge the world and reward exploration. Since implementing these, players found the flow of these quests more natural and were able to construct pahts through them. This solved the issue without compromising the game's overall sublimity.

Some changes were deliberately not made, in order to preserve the game's identity. For example, a player indicated desire for an in-game map of the Prison, while also noting the feeling of being lost. The fact is, the feeling of being lost is an objective of the Prison's design, which would not be so strongly felt with a map; sosmall inconveniences to the player are sometimes necessary in order to achieve a greater total effect. An in-game map is a marker of the "completion" of exploration within an area, which is absolutely antithetical to sublime design. In regards to a suitable balance, I should focus on having areas that are not so confusing that the absence of a map causes more harm than good, and this is a feature that must be balanced with the indistinct boundaries.

The issue of darkness was difficult becaus the sublime experience is best with a specific light level in areas like the Prison, which is different depending on the player's device, and I cannot rely upon the player to make that judgement themselves if offering a brightness slider as they will usually go for the brightest settings, which makes the game easiest, to the detriment of the emotional quality of the game's levels. The darkness was consistently found scary, this fear being a feature of the sublime. I enhanced this fear by actually adding strange new scary enemies in the darkness, so forever making the player afraid of what might be lurking ahead unseen.

Reflection

The emotional effect of the Prison, in its atmosphere and level design, was particularly successful throughout all tests. Players enjoyed its size, which is first hidden from the player, until further exploration; players also enjoyed the Prison's secrets: it is clear the Prison's design is succesfully evokative of the sublime. I did observe that all players traversed the Prison differently. The design of the Prison and Tower Atrium also appeared to have the effect of aesthetic sublimity. It was further observed that the sparsity of Fountains created trepidation; this fera associated with the sublime, moreover linking with Martin's assocation of the sublime and difficulty.

Players were consistently most excited when discovering hidden elements and making progress in quests. This always paired with a sense of surprise, which exists only because of the inexplicit nature of all these things. I do not believe that the feeling of excitement would be as great if the path to these things was straighforward. Also players who saw the provisonal list of objectives were drawn to those that were an alternative outcome of a quest that they had completed, or which occur in a location that they had not found, confirming the game's replayability potential.

I found it difficult to test how well the longevity of the sublime is supported owing to the short test sessions. The best I could do in this regard was to have some users test multiple times. There were then conclusions that could be exclusively drawn frmo these tests. I did find that when players returned to the game a second time, they always discovered things that they had not previously, and were excited by them.

Nevertheless, the issue sustained pertaining to the limited scale and content of the game, that isnot on the surface indicative of a sublime experience, so affecting the player's mode of interaction. Results of the questionnaire exposed that players often viewed the world as a singular and immutable crafted unit rather than an unfathomable and dynamic sublime whole. This was combatted by adding more content at the start of the game that the player is likely to encounter, with these all intending to open up the mechanical scope of the game. When the same player played multiple different times, they were excited by the emergence of new events, which they had not discovered before.

Players had different natural inclinations. The questionnaire reveals that this might be due to their exposure to different kinds of games, which value different styles of play. Given the same build and same objective, some players would focus entirely on entering the Tower, and others would carefully explore Gatetown. This is a good outcome as it allows observant players to be specially rewarded. These dispositions informed by different game experiences could help or hinder their interaction with the game; I hypothesise that many players tried to apply their existing mental models from other games to this one, which could make exploration more or less effective. But by interrupting this model with the addition of new events in hte earliest sections of hte game I found that players' approaches shifted to be more explorative and consider the world as less linear.

Going forwards, I am interested in verifying that the sublime can be further elevated by adding more content and extending the existing mechanics. Indeed the features utilised by the game seem to progress towards that end, and from the tests it is strongly suggested they would suffice for a sublime game given enough time and resources.

As far as my discoveries can be broadly applied, I believe that individual design features can be implemented in other games as many have done across genres, inasmuch as it makes sense within the game's vision. Many games have a mechanical focus upon mastery, where the enjoyment is in the player's mastering of the system, in which case it does not make sense to have a system that deliberately resists understanding. Other features like invisible choices, dynamic environments, indistinct boundaries, are useful in many games, but especially when there is more focus on the world than mechanics.

What I learned

The contention between the ambitous scope of the project and the limited timeframe posed one of the largest challenges throughout, and my feedback on the assessment advises a more thorough consideration of scope and feasibility in the planning stages of a project.
