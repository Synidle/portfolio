The Tower is a videogame demo developed independently as a final project in university, with conception, planning, and development taking around eight months in total. The demo is approximately two hours in playtime, with rudimentary assets, mechanics, and content, with the focus of realising the core idea behind the ambitious project within the short timeframe.

Design

The aim of the project was to realise and sustain a sensation of sublime in a 3D action roleplaying game. That is to say, the sense of awe and wonder felt by players of excellent roleplaying games. The efficacy of both The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion (Bethesda Game Studios, 2006) and Dark Souls (FromSoftware, 2011) in this realm has been analysed by Martin (2011) and Vella (2015) respectively; and the foundation for this project draws upon that literature whilst critiquing and developing upon it. To achieve this aim, I was prepared to question and forego design conventions as was sensible according to hypotheses, and then rigorously test the effect of design decisions with real users.

The approach taken adopts Vella's observation of obfuscation of a game's precise working supporting sublime's longevity, while recognising that if such is done too liberally it has the opposite effect.

There were many unique and interesting mechanics I implemented as a means of achieving my aim, not all of which I will discuss in order to protect my ideas. However there are minor yet very effective features that I will explain.

One way in which The Tower achieves sublimity while avoiding extreme obscurity is in its destructible environment pieces. Dark Souls and Elden Ring (FromSoftware, 2022) hide areas, so expanding their worlds beyond that visible, by using illusory walls: walls appearing normal, but on interaction disappearing. In offline mode, there is no indication a wall is illusory, nor even that they exist; yet the player messages left in online mode make them too transparent and so effectively redundant, while also offering other player advice that can diminish the sense of discovery. The player essentially has to pick the lesser of these two "evils" — both of which hurt the ability to effectuate sublimity. In The Tower, destructible environment pieces are similarly used to hide areas or connexion points, but in a manner not too transparent nor to obscure. The player is most likely sent to the Prison early in the game, wherein they are required to destroy a wall to progress, with there being implicit indicators that the wall can be destroyed. This awakens the player to the mechanic, which they associate with the crumbling environment they begin the Prison section in —and so in other such environments of the Prison, there are other destructible elements. Others are less explicit, but in no case could the player never have possibly suspected that the element could be destroyed. Destructible columns also offer a similar yet different experience, by bridging two visible areas, and so informing the player that visible yet seemingly inaccessible areas can be accessed by creative means, so broadening their conception of the game in much the same way.

Another consideration was how to save. The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion etc. allow the player to manually save at any moment, which trivialises many gameplay aspects, and makes moment-to-moment gameplay less tense, with less emotional investment. Meanwhile, Dark Souls etc. use particular checkpoints. The problem with Dark Souls' system is that the game state is saved continuously, with only the player's position returning to the checkpoint, and minor enemies returning. So items consumed before dying remain consumed after respawning. I find that this discourages the use of items, and by extension experimentation. My solution was using Fountains as the only points at which progress is saved, also using them as points of fast-travel to other Fontains to facilitate back-and-forth in the open-world. Refer back to my commitment that all mechanics should expand rather than restrict the player's conception of possibility within the game: allowance of experimentation comes under this.

Unplanned mechanics were introduced as testing revealed them necessary. For example, I initiaully did not want to have a "running" movement option because I find different movement speeds are often redundant features, and I do not want the player to rush through the game; however in order to have such large environments I would need to grant the plaeyr faster movement. I did not want to just increase the base movement as this would make the player too fast in tight areas; I also did not want to introduce additional input complexity with a new feature so I linked the run with the existing dash mechanic. There was then the issue of how running should affect stamina. Given running existed to allow the player to efficiently cross large environments, a draining stamina gauge should not inhibit this, but also an increase of speed should not be free. I like the systems of The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind (Bethesda Game Studios, 2002) and Elden Ring where running is free in the environment alone but has a penalty in combat. These systems however are unimmersive: it is not intuitive for running to drain stamina yet continue to function without stamina in The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind, and Elden Ring's system of only draining stamina in combat feels ad hoc. My system is better: there is a fixed stamina cost in the initial dash, and running after that does not drain stamina but nor does it regenerate while the player continues to run. This makes running costless while exploring but costly in combat — and without inbuilt inconsistency in the mechanic.

An issue with the game succeeding The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind is the use of quest-markers and too explicit directions, which dissolves the uncertainty of ways in which to progress a quest, and also discouraging interaction with the world in favour of directly pursuing the quest-marker. Quests in The Tower are therefore made navigable using diegetic information and player intuition alone. But acknowledging that a degree of obscurity is necessary for a sublime game, some quests are made harder to navigate than those common in The Elder Scrolls III: Morrowind. For instance, there are no explicit directions in the quest related to the Dragon, and no indication that the Dragon Covenant Claw is the key to unlock the Dragon Gaol except for the subtle environment hint of the markings on the pedestal also appearing on the claw. A necessary consequence is that the player might chase red herrings: by following their own intuition the player is likely to suspect the existence of quests or paths that do not exist. However the possibility of this is necessary in the sublime experience, even if an undesirable consequence.

Another invaluable element to sublime quest design is invisible choices, those being consequential actions that are not explicitly presented to the player as such, which are explained by Game Maker's Toolkit (2022). The clearest invisible choice is freeing the Dragon. This is not an option explicitly presented to the player, but one that requires a particular activity and prior preparation to be completed, with many individual "invisible" factors: first finding the Dragon, of whom little is mentioned in-game, and acquiring the key to activate the door; then finding a weapon capable of cutting the chain; and finally the outcome is determined by the player's dialogue with the Dragon and certain attribute values, and whether the player possesses the Dragon Covenant Claw that enables allyship with the Dragon. But beyond this, there are even more invisible choices in hte game, which have consequences far more difficult to place.

Moreover, situations that inconvenience the player are deliberately formed, as they invoke the player to consider there being a more efficient solution. For example, the Moonstone Scythe is required to break the Dragon's chains —this weapon requires two hands to wield and so the player must forgo their torch in this area of pitch-darkness; also within the Prison however, a hood granting illuminated vision can be acquired from a mage, and this can be known to the player as the mage's line of vision is clearly illuminated. This mindset of their being more advantageous strategies will then persist outside of these inconveniences to all gameplay moments: so the player becomes sublimely aware that their current experience is not summative of the game's possibilities.

Planning

The game's world, story, unique mechanics, and so forth, were all carefully considered with the purpose of achieving the sublime. These were implemented in rudimentary form, tested independently, then tested again in carefully designed user tests, to evaluate efficacy and inform the next development sprint within an agile methodology.

The initial planning approach was the early definition of a Minimum Viable Product (MVP) to establish the ideas in a small project, with a Gantt chart used to project and maintain progress. However I found it useful to adopt a more agile and iterative approach, whereby I defined successive intermediate versions spaced one or two weeks apart, each having its own design goal, implementing features in a basic manner, being evaluated upon, sometimes wiht the assitance of other users, then informing the plan for the next intermediate version. The aim was to ensure both consistent progress, and that all progress was made with the fianl goal in mind. Each intermediate version as far as the MVP was outlined first before starting development, with flexibility permitted. The Gantt chart continued to be used as an overarching monitor of progress.

In conjunction with development, a Task Tracker was used to formalise individual tasks, permitting also notes on bugs and opportunities for improvement and extension.

Development

The game was developed in the Godot Engine using the C# programming language. While Godot encourages the use of its bespoke GDScript, I chose C# as my scripting language as it is strongly typed, faster, and more universal.

A notable reason for choosing Godot is that its Node system stimulates compositional practices. Furthermore, my scripting approach favoured composition over inheritance, which affords a greater flexibility perfect for a sublime game that resists formulae or simple, intuitable structures. Specifically, an entity within the game can comprise as many of the implemented mechanics as I wish, rather than those mechanics being restricted by its class. This reflects that same quality of the game's lack of a build system.

For example, having Door and Lock as separate classes, both of which reference but do not inherit the Interactable class, enables more possibilities regarding what can be locked and how they can be nlocked. This inables the disconnect between the door leading to the Dragon Gaol and its activator that requires a key, with the activator subtly hinting the requirement of the Covenant Claw: the door itself gives no suggestion of requiring a special keya nd so would leave the player confused, especially considering how many keys are in the game. The fact that the clase is not a conventional key means it will not unlock a conventional door, so the astute player will think of this activator.

When facing the issue of how to allow the player to save their progress at the start of the Prison while not having that Fountain checkpoint offer transportation out of the Prison at this stage, but allowing it later for the benefit of returning for quests, I made use of this mechanic again in another context: having the transportation capabilities of the fontain "locked" until external activation.

Level Design

The forefront consideration of world and level design was Vella's concept of "indistinct boundaries" extracted from Dark Souls' own world design, contrasted against the transparent and liberal openness of The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion's open world.

I intuited from the literature and my own experiences of games that a world seemingly linear that branches out, with difficulty, and only at close inspection, is more sublime, and more able to surprise and inspire wonder, than a world that is transparently open, so laying all its cards down at once. Furthermore, a truncated linear main path embellished by branching content befits a highly replayable game that allows the player to discover more content each playthrough and tread paths that they did not on their first playthrough, perhaps because they were "evil" — i.e. discouraged in the narrative —but which nevertheless seemed interesting. Replayability is necessary for a sublime game: a game is not leveraging sublimity if the player, having finished the game once, is aware that they missed content, but is not inclined to play the game again to encounter new content; a game is most sublime if a player can continue returning, discovering new content, and so making the concept of the game in their mind seemingly infinite.

It would seem obvious for a sublime game to have a huge world, and a lengthy, epic story; but long main-questlines usually have only one route through, or very minor variations thereupon. The Tower aims to have completely different paths through the main story depending upon the factions joined, which is possible with a shorter story. This makes a single playthrough far less representative of the game's scope of possibility. This feeds into the game's consistent encouragement of replay, allowing new events to emerge and so expanding the sublime horizon observed by the player.

To ensure the designing of areas consistent with the overarching aim, prior to sketching level layouts I listed level aims for the particular level in its own text document. This would also outline how the level fits into the rest of the game, and how it should impact the player emotionally. Therefore all areas contribute in their way to the gestalt sublime experience. Moreover, moodboards were uesd for each area in order to capture a particular aesthetic.

Levels were then sketched, then roughly blocked-out using primitive 3D shapes for a sense of scale, space, and design, while being easy to reconfigure to achieve the perfect layout. When content with the layout following thorough testing, I would gradually increase the fidelity with more textures and 3D models. For example, I was able to pull out the bridge leading into the Tower, making the outline of the town more interesting. Such a change was easy given the town was in the early stages of blocking-out, but would have been difficult were the town already high-fidelity. Moreover, I was able to easily introduce new interconnexions of paths in the Prison.

Modelling

Blender 4.3 was used for modelling and animation, and Aseprite, Krita, and Paint.NET for textures.

The game employs a retro early-3D aesthetic for numerous reasons. The association with classic RPGs implicitly conjures expectations of less "hand-holding". Furthermore, given the game's scale, lower-fidelity assets both permits production of more assets within a shorter time and the presence of more assets for less computational power. Finally, lower-quality assets more disconncted from realistic representation greater ignite the player's imagination; for this reason a low resolution is also used for a "grainy" effect that disguises model and textural imperfections — this is used in conjunction with a subtle noise filter. It is great that the player can observe distant locations and be able to wonder what they are, rather than instantly being able to make out details.

The choice to use a retro 4:3 aspect ratio does more than just provide authenticity to the style: it should improve the gameplay experience. The visual display is the primary means of communication from the game world to the player, particularly regarding world and level design; hence the player's subjectivity of the game is informed by the organisation of assets within the immediate display. A widescreen display is appropriate for most open-world or action games — particularly those that are in third-person —as it shows those features around the player horizontally; but in the case of The Tower, there is a very central focus, that being a vertical tower: an aspect ratio narrower than that of our eyes forces more focus on what is above and below the player, implicitly supporting vertical level design. A widescreen aspect ratio would encourage a more traditional open world game akin to The Elder Scrolls IV: Oblivion rather than encouraging a focussed, vertical, indistinct level design akin to Dark Souls.

Furthermore the game targets 30 frames per second rather than the standard 60 or higher in order to reinforce an aversion to strict combat execution challenge. I want the game to be more conceptually and intellectually challenging, and high skill requirements are another cause for the player to seek external help through guides in order to find manageable routes to overcome the challenge, causing a dissolution of the sublime by making explicit game information.
