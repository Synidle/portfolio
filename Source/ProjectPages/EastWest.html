Artistic objects are products of designers' ways of thinking (Hutchinson), and one component of "harmony" is conformity: Japanese's propensity to internalise emotions invites emotionally-complex characters in videogames, through whom emotion can be acknowledged (Kalata), e.g. in Persona. Interdependence also manifests in how Eastern games centralise relationships for motivation: how characters motivate the protagonist (Hutchinson) but also how the player is motivated, as ... in Shadow of the Colossus.

Given ideological basis for differences in design, it follows that these are not incidental nor intentional, thus not arbitrary nor mutable, but deeply cultural. Where former analysis encourages culturalisation towards global monoculture, this new framing encourages cultural preservation beneficial to intransigence between East and West, and their individual merits.

Invocation of mukokuseki is a detriment for applying intentionality to games' cultural quality, divisively defining games as either mukokuseki or "self-Orientalising". ... Theories drawing on mukokuseki consider games' presentation deliberately bland or exoticised for global markets, ignoring earnest presentation of culture, fallaciously considering all expression of culture intentional rather than emergent, furthermore rendering normative Western culture. ... Mukokuseki also assumes that Japanese games are successful globally because of the absence of certain qualities, rather than qualities' presence...

The problem is that Western egalitarianism believes that everything should be for everyone. O'Hagan explores Ico's Japanese essence with a view to culturalising it — making it palatable — for a global audience. But even as a Japanese game, Ico was unconventional; features O'Hagan identified were not Japanese idiosyncrasies but products of careful, intentional design. Western egalitarianism accepts differences in design insofar as they are palatably and immediately identifiable as exotic, or fit within a monocultural system. O'Hagan praises Ico's non-verbal storytelling because it fits into a monocultural order without language barriers, but all other features are considered expendable. Non-verbal storytelling cannot be normalised within Japanese games, which rely upon nuances in dialogue that do not exist in English to establish hierarchies and reveal personalities (Hutchinson): cultural homogeneity prevents that acute resonance of games intended for a specific culture.

What I learned

Through my research and in the writing of this essay, I came to appreciate the cultural justifications behind game design, and grew a passion for preserving culture within the videogame medium. I became aware that these cultural imprints are not innocuous but important parts of games' identities.
