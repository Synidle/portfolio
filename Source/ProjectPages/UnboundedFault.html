Unbounded Fault was a game made as a group of four for a university project, with myself as Project Manager, and also taking some design and programming responsibilities. I received a grade of 89 for this project, which stood out for its "scale and varying experience". Especially, the number and quality of individual aspects, and how they interacted to complete the experience, were commended, affirming my success in conducting the team.

As a result of this project, I became more comfortable working within Unity, and importantly also creating "complete" experiences: here I had been forced to schedule development towards a coherent product.

Responsibilities

As Project Manager, my primary responsibilities were project-scheduling, assessing work quality, and assigning tasks.

I also chaired all of the meetings, and produced agendas.

I also did the mechanics side of programming, and designed programming implementations through diagrams, and enforced good programming practice upon the team, such as use of singletons and prefabs, to ensure the project's scalability.

As the final point of quality assurance, I frequently had to test the game and point out issues in meetings, which would then result in more tasks being allocated; then some fixes I would implement myself if they could not be done to standard on time, so as to maintain the project's momentum.

I also had a hand in theme, narrative, and mechanics design, and proposed amendments to the level design, implementing them with the Lead Designer's approval.

When it came to presenting our project, I developed a plan to ensure all necessary items would be covered and within an appropriate timeframe. I assigned the appropriate slides to each member; I presented the development process, which I was responsible for, and made concluding remarks. I led rehearsals of the presentation and ammended it according to members' confidence and to fit within the time constraint.

Planning

I constructed a project timeline divided into week-long sprints in accordance with the agile software development methodology I had chosen for enabling us to rapidly produce a viable product and then iteratively improve upon it, responding to issues identified. This timeline was illustrated on a Gantt chart.

I divided the timeline into distinct phases, immediately establishing soft deadlines lest too much time be spent on any one aspect, so ensuring optimal completion of all aspects.

Of the development phases, Part One concerned output of a Minimal Viable Product, which I tasked the Design Lead with describing; Part Two focussed on refinement.

I also maintained a tickets document of programming tasks, tracking who was assigned a task, its due date, its current status, and if applicable an ammended due date.

The fact that all members had clearly defined roles made meeting discussion easier, as one member would be able to have a final say in any decision. I also advised all members to read the agenda prior to the meeting so that they would be aware of its items and direction, so being prepared for an efficient meeting. I decided the roles in a meeting in which each of us pitched our individual skillsets, so I was confident at this stage of members' suiting to their roles.

Together, at a very early stage, we divised a code of conduct for professionalism and efficiency, and I held all members to this code of conduct fiercely, noting when work was not completed on time or to quality, or when members did not follow their responsibilities.

Design

Under my guidance the team established basic cornerstones for our design:

  • Simple mechanics & gameplay loop; easy to pick up and enjoy.
  • Replayable.

The basis from which our design stemmed came from two other members: one who suggested a focus on gameplay over impressive graphics or narrative, and another who suggested a move to simplicity and fun from the contemporary prevelence of over-complexity. From this basis we also adopted the association with retro-games: artefacts of a simpler time when games were easy to pick up and play.

To ensure the game was easy to pick up, I recommended commitment to a restricted control scheme mirroring the controls offered by the classic Nintendo Entertainment System controller. The intention is that the game can be played with a retro controller or on an arcade machine, with the player learning the controls simply by pressing all of the buttons. For the benefit of accessibility I also ensured there are two methods of keyboard input, so suiting both right- and left-handed players.

As a means of capturing and cultivating design ideas towards a complete picture, I had all team members research games they thought could provide inspiration, and also construct a moodboard each to aid us with theme and aesthetics.

Collectively, within the design we endeavoured to ensure that, as far as possible, game mechanics could be understood organically, rather than being told explicitly in text. One of my implementations towards this was an ambush of spiders after the player picks up the Curved Sword. The player will instinctively swing the sword to kill the spiders, and in doing so realise the sword can cut the cobwebs that had been slowing the player's movement up to this point.

My contribution to the replayability aspect was my idea to pair the strategy required in challenging games with strong, intentional level design with the adaptability required in games with random elements. While the structure of the dungeon remains constant, select enemy encounters, in addition to dungeon loot, are randomised — so preventing the formulation of an ensured strategy, rather always requiring some adaptability on the part of the player. Another effect of this is encouraging the player to explore new routes or use new items if their hitherto strategy had failed: this was made possible through weapons with different abilities, enemies that require distinct approaches to defeat, and different paths offering different challenges.

An example of this, is that the Mimic enemies in the first half of the level have a small chance of dropping the Curved Sword, otherwise located down a specific in the second half; so in the second half of the level wherein the Curved Sword is incredibly useful, players fortunate enough to already have found it in the first half will be motivated to explore other paths.

I also assisted in devising a narrative that echoed the "replayability" focus and the randomisation aspect: the player character is a scientist trapped in a series of parallel universes of his own unfortunate creation, with each death transporting him to another alternate world where things are slightly different.

Development

Given the small size of our group it was expected that all of us be able to pitch in within any area of development or documentation. I clarified that the Lead Designer and Lead Developer would manage the design and development sides respectively, sometimes having another member work under them in this regard, relieving me of having to manage everything and so allowing me to focus on scheduling and final quality assurance. This would also ensure that I would not need to minutely inspect all work, assuming it had already been inspected by the relevant leader in that area. When working remotely, the Design and Development leads would facilitate means of uploading work, collaborating, and checking work.

Meetings were held bi-weekly: mid-sprint and at the culmination of each sprint. The objecties were to check progress and allocate tasks, and for members to raise concerns, or suggestions for how to progress in the next sprint. At the start of every meeting, and whenever I observed the project, I ensured that the project was satisfactory according to the design brief and completed consistent to the timetable; if dissatisfactory I discussed the issue with the whole team and revised members' tasks and priorities to revitalise momentum; following repeated issues I would speak to the member individually and then the project supervisor if the issue persisted. However, it was established I would only be the last point of quality assurance, with one member tasked with particularly rigorous testing of all newly-implemented features, and with each member also expected to thoroughly test each of their additions themselves.

Meetings were sometimes an opportunity for the entire group to play through the game together and discuss its state, and how it might be improved in the next sprint. Meetings also enabled reflection on the group's methodology, discussion of challenges faced by any members, and the addressing of these challenges or motivation to work harder.

The Lead Designer fulfilled his responsibility well, especially when it came to other members producing assets and implementing the level based on his designs, which he could check remotely through images and video. Giving him the ability to allocate tasks allowed us to achieve a much richer and more professional game with more and higher-quality assets than if he had been required to complete all this alone.

For the programming part, I required designs be made for different components and their relationships before them being programmed, so having modular code that could be easily repurposed, and having minimal impact on the functionality of other code, so improving development efficiency. The production of UML diagrams to outline a careful and in tentional modular design also enabled collaboration between members.

To ensure scalability, efficiency, and collaboration, I required adoption of aspects of Extreme Programming methodology: collective ownership of code and assets, with improvements being made as soon as a problem was identified, with scripts were refactored to improve functionality or modularity. This improved long-term programming efficiency and scalability.

One of my many contributions to the code was the randomisation system. I wrote a Randomisation singleton with public methods providing randomisation to diverse aspects of the game. Randomised enemy encounters were made possible through a RandomSpawner script providing an interface through which one can form different encounters, with each being given a chance value of occurring as a decimal from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain). Thus, it was easy for other members to create random encounters, so allowing programming and level implementation to be separate activities completed concurrently by different members.

Challenges

When meeting discussions moved off-topic, or used as opportunities to do work rather than discuss it, I would move everyone on to the next item and reiterate the purpose of hte meeting and each individual's responsibilities, sometimesr ecommending further discussion after the meeting. It was crucial that all items on the agenda be addressed, so while there was room for discussion, it was important to remain focussed.

At first it was difficult to assign tasks to one member who did not claim to have any particular strengths. To begin with I had him work on research and writing tasks for our design documentation and reports, at which he was not great; then I found he was very competent at testing, taking minutes, and finding or producing assets, so made these his primary responsibilities, resulting in improved pace and quality at this point.

In regards to game features, I found that the "dash" mechanic alone was insufficient to avoid damage, so I implemented it that if the player dashed within a short time-frame after taking damage, they would regain the health lost. Such revisions and new implementations were possible owing to the early output of the MVP and consistent output of a viable product after every subsequent sprint under my guidance.

Evaluation

The flexibility enabled by our agile methodology led to a smooth development process and a higher-quality product.

Meetings were effective. They were held twice a week, which was found to be perfect for keeping track of work and assigning further tasks. They were usually productive and had a good atmosphere. This was particularly because we had daily communication remotely, so we were generally aware of progress even prior to the meeting; so the meetings served as a formal review of work and task allocation, and for constructive discussion.

Friction and fecklessness within the team could have been avoided, I realised, by having ramifications encoded into our code of conduct from the beginning, for failure to meet expectations. Without equal passion put into the project among us, diligent members were often required to pick up others' slack. Later making clear that members would be rewarded or penalised for good or poor effort did result in members gaining the motivation to work harder.

As a result of this project, I now have appreciation for the challenges of collaboration, and have discovered methods of mitigating issues: including also how to develop in a modular way to avoid conflict, and how to write code and systems that are easy for others to utilise and understand.

I now have better understanding of how to manage a team. If I am to manage again, I will more strictly enforce deadlines and quality-control. I believe my scheduling for the project was good, as we were able to produce a lengthy, complete experience with numerous features and mechanics and without having greater work-burden towards the end of the project. However, ultimately the group fell quite behind the envisoned schedule, resulting in a poorer product than I would have liked.

The occasional missed deadline was expected and factored into flexible scheduling. However, I should have been stricter when missed deadlines and poor-quality work became a common issue, whereas this happened to infrequently and too late in the process. I believe that had the other members been more motivated, we would have a much higher-quality and feature-rich product. Reprimanding failures would also have been easier if there were clearer documentation of missed deadlines and incomplete work.
